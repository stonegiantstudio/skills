# Test results, 2026-10-20 (scenario-fernwick)

We sent each draft request to Claude Sonnet 5.5 through the API with nothing
else in context, five drafts per arm. The arms:

- **control** — no skill;
- **pre-1.6** — `main` before this release (1.5.0 plus the false-agent pair
  test from #21, which never shipped as a version);
- **1.6.0** — the text this release ships.

A separate agent graded every draft blind: it saw the fact sheet, the checks,
and the drafts, never which arm wrote which. The table counts the drafts that
failed each check, out of five.

from another domain, so the 1.6.0 column doesn't reward a writer for
recognizing the test.

**Clause lists.** The skill asks the writer to deliver the false-agent pair
test's clause list. A keyword match on the writers' notes, which the grader
never saw, found the list in 0 of 5 pre-1.6 drafts and 5 of 5 1.6.0 drafts.

## How we got here

Two earlier rounds, each graded by its own blind agent, decided which edits
to keep:

- **Round 2** compared control, pre-1.6, and the first edit: 15, 16, and 6
  failures.
- **Round 3** compared the first and second edits under a grader told to
  count a number or a post that draws a conclusion as a false agent: 9 and
  5 failures.

The graders differed in strictness, so compare numbers within a round, not
across rounds. The final run above compares all three arms under one grader.

## What each edit answers

Each quote below comes from a control or pre-1.6 writer unless it says
otherwise.

- **Writing a fresh draft (the provenance sheet).** Four control writers and
  two pre-1.6 writers in round 2 invented specifics. Some narrated a scene
  that never happened ("Most of us have tried the Sunday cram…"). One
  promised "the October figures" and quoted "the question we hear most".
  Two writers on the first edit attached the all-session median to the
  learners who reviewed most days, which round 2 counted under check 1. The
  second edit names that direction under "Stretched figures".
- **Absolutes about people.** Three pre-1.6 writers in round 2 made a
  "nobody" claim, for example "Nobody has to decide whether they have an
  hour to spare."
- **Stretched figures.** A pre-1.6 writer turned a recall figure that covers
  only learners who reviewed on 25 or more days of 30 into "five minutes of
  review a day is enough to keep most of the words you learn."
- **Didactic framing.** Two pre-1.6 writers followed a volunteered failure
  with a moral: "We learned what asking too much costs," and "the lesson is
  the same one."
- **Data as speaker.** Writers in every arm made a number the speaker: "the
  number tells us," "our data can't separate the two," "the 81% does not
  promise."
- **Verbless summary line.** One writer on the first edit closed on a
  verbless line, which round 2 counted under check 7 (spec shorthand).
- **No entry added.** Mood softeners and spec shorthand failed at most once
  in any baseline arm, so we added no entries for them.

## What still fails

All five 1.6.0 writers still made a number or the post the speaker:

- in headings: "What our numbers say", "What September showed";
- in a post describing itself: "This post explains why we think that is the
  right size";
- in a figure's limits: "the 81% doesn't prove it".

We stopped after three rounds of edits, the limit we set before testing
began. The next edit to try is a line in the pair test telling the writer to
read headings and limitation sentences as clauses too, with the move for a
limitation: "from that figure, we can't tell whether…".

One 1.6.0 writer invented a detail ("We had spent months making the daily
session as small as we could").

## Limits of this test

We used five drafts per arm, one model, and one fictional product, and we
didn't measure how much graders vary. The round 2 grader and the final
grader scored the same ten baseline drafts at 31 and 35 failures in total,
so expect a few failures of noise either way.
